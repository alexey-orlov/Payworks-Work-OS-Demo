## Executive Summary

This is the first substantive conversation ever recorded with this account, and the first first-hand expense evidence from a small employer. Client services requested it directly: expenses had come up twice on the quarterly check-in and been taken away twice without a follow-up — *"the reason I asked for this is you two mentioned expenses to me on the quarterly check-in, twice, and both times I said 'I'll take that back,' so I'm taking it back."* — Dean Murphy. Nothing was demonstrated; the whole ~14 minutes was current-state discovery, and it was unusually dense.

The account is 75 employees (76 as of the Monday before the call), one office plus six fully-remote staff and two field sales people. Two people run everything people-related, and expenses sit entirely with one of them: *"Just me. I do payroll, I do expenses, I do the benefits enrolment, I do the T4s."* — Their Payroll Administrator. Their own description of the split — *"I do everything with a number in it. She does everything with a feeling in it. That's the split."* — is worth keeping, because the two roles disagreed productively for most of the call and the disagreement is the most useful material in it.

This call focused on five areas: (1) the current claim-to-reimbursement flow, (2) submission discipline and chasing, (3) approval thresholds — where the two attendees disagreed and then converged, (4) mobile, where they disagreed and did **not** fully converge, and (5) the buy-versus-build maths that killed a standalone expense tool last year.

**Current flow.** A claim starts on an Excel file on the shared drive: *"It's an Excel file, it lives on the shared drive, it's called Expense Claim Template and it has the year in the filename which is a whole problem in itself."* The year in the filename is a live cost — *"people have last year's on their desktop. So I get claims on the 2024 form with the old mileage rate on it and I have to redo the math."* Routing is inconsistent by design accident: *"they fill in the form, they attach the receipts, and they email it to me. Or to their manager and then to me, depending on who taught them, which is not consistent."* Volume is ~25 claims and ~$6,000 a month from the same 15–20 people, with one ~$14,000 month a year when sales attend a conference; a single trip claim can be $4,000 — *"That's a quarter of my monthly volume in a single email."* The whole cycle costs her *"A day and a half a month. And half of that is chasing."*

**Receipts are digital here, and that corrected a live design assumption.** Asked whether receipts are paper, the answer was emphatic: *"Hardly ever paper. I'd say — of the ones I got last month, maybe three were photos of a paper receipt. Everything else was a PDF or somebody forwarding me the confirmation email."* The reason is the spend mix — *"It's a conference registration, or it's a client dinner where the restaurant emails you the receipt, or it's somebody's put a software subscription on their personal Visa."* Michelle named the assumption this breaks on the call: *"I've been spending a lot of time with a customer where the whole thing is people photographing paper in a truck, and I think I'd started assuming that was the problem."*

**Invisible recurring spend is the pain nobody owns.** Personal-card software subscriptions arrive as monthly expense claims and never get re-decided: *"I counted in the spring, we had eleven of those. Eleven recurring subscriptions coming through as personal expense claims."* — and *"they'd been approved — a manager clicked yes on the first one twenty-two months ago. Nobody's looked since. It's four hundred dollars a month of subscriptions that nobody's decided on."* The mechanism is the amount: *"It's invisible because it's twenty-nine dollars at a time. Nothing under fifty dollars gets looked at twice, ever."*

**Submission discipline.** A 60-day rule exists and is never enforced — *"I've never once enforced it. Because what am I going to do, not pay somebody back for a hotel?"* — leaving claims that span four months and cross a closed quarter. The ask is a system-owned nudge, and the reason is social rather than operational: *"If the system nudged them at day fifteen — not me, the system — I think ninety percent of it goes away"*, because *"right now the reminder is from me, and then I'm the nag, and that's a relationship cost in a company this size. You see these people at lunch."*

**Approval thresholds — the headline.** Today the policy is one manager approval for everything, with a $5,000 owner tier that has fired twice ever (the conference and a trade-show booth). The Payroll Administrator asked for a $250 auto-approval floor: *"what I would want is a number — say two hundred and fifty dollars — and under that, it just goes. No approval. Straight to me, straight onto the pay."* Her argument is that the current control is fictional — *"right now a manager is clicking yes on a nineteen dollar parking receipt, and they're not reading it… So it's a fake control that costs me three days of waiting."* The HR Manager pushed back with the stronger argument: the approval email's value is not control, it is awareness. *"They're not reading, but they are seeing. That's the real reason. The thing the manager gets out of that email isn't a control, it's information."* Her example is concrete — *"if one of my people is expensing parking three times a week, I want to notice that. Not to stop it. To go, oh, are you driving to a client every day?"* — and her objection to auto-approval is that *"that entire signal disappears from the manager's world."* Michelle reframed it as approval-versus-visibility and both accepted the same resolution: under $250 the claim pays without approval and the manager is **told rather than asked**. The Payroll Administrator's only condition: *"That works for me too, if it genuinely doesn't hold the money."*

**And the notification shape is a hard requirement, not a detail.** The HR Manager refused email outright — *"Told, but not in an email, please. I get a hundred and something emails a day and a 'for your information' email is a deleted email."* — and specified the alternative: *"I'd want it as a list. Like, when I go in to do something else, there's a 'here's what your team spent this month' and I look at it once. That's a much better shape than twenty emails."* Both then realised the list solves the subscription blind spot: *"eleven twenty-nine-dollar things in a list looks like something. Eleven separate emails over a month looks like nothing."*

**Where an approved claim lands: payroll, not accounts payable.** *"On the pay. It's always been on the pay."* There is effectively no A/P function for staff reimbursements — *"I do that too, but for staff reimbursements it goes on the pay because it's one less thing."* Payroll is semi-monthly, 15th and last business day, cutoff two business days before, so the wait is bounded: *"Never more than two weeks and usually less. Nobody complains about the speed. Genuinely, that's not one of my problems."* This is the direct opposite of the Northwind fact the VP2 page currently designs against, and the attendee named the reason herself: *"I can imagine it would be if you're running it through A/P on a different cycle."*

**Mobile split the room and stayed split.** Submission on a phone is wanted — *"If I'm at a conference and I've just paid for a taxi, I want to deal with it there… snapping the taxi receipt and being done with it, yes."* Approval on a phone is actively opposed, on evidence from their own time-off rollout: *"We put time-off approvals on the phone and the approval rate went to basically a hundred percent overnight, and it wasn't because everybody's requests got better."* The mechanism she describes is physical — *"It's the same tap as clearing a notification. Your thumb does it before your brain arrives."* She would accept mobile approval under $250 (*"under two-fifty she's right and it shouldn't need approving anyway"*) but wants a large claim **blocked**, not warned: *"the four-thousand-dollar conference claim? No… I'd rather the system just didn't let them do it on a phone at all"* — because *"'discouraged' means a grey box that says 'are you sure' and everyone taps yes on those too."* Michelle noted the same instinct had reached her from a finance person at a much larger company via the account team, which the HR Manager summarised as *"It's a thumbs thing."*

**Buy-versus-build, and why bundling wins here.** They evaluated a standalone expense tool last year at about $9 a head — roughly $8,000 a year for 75 people — and rejected it on two grounds. The first was ROI arithmetic: *"the owner asked me how many hours it saves, and I said maybe a day a month, and a day of my time is not eight thousand dollars."* The second is the one that matters to us: *"And then I'd still be exporting it into the payroll to get it onto the pay, so I'd have bought a second system and kept the manual step. That was the actual killer."* The HR Manager drew the pricing conclusion unprompted: *"Whereas if it's part of what we already pay you for, and it's just — there, in the thing we already use — that's a completely different conversation."*

**Opportunity Areas.** (1) The threshold + told-not-asked + monthly team-spend list is a coherent, small, buildable package that both roles have already agreed to — it is the closest thing VP2 has to a validated slice. (2) Reimbursement-on-payroll is a native advantage against standalone tools for this segment, and it is exactly the step that killed the incumbent evaluation. (3) The pay-statement claim breakdown is a cheap payroll-side fix with an explicit willingness-to-pay attached. (4) Both attendees committed to a 30-minute prototype review, which converts this account from a persona into a usable design partner.

**Strategy / Hypothesis Validation.** The `expense-management-vp2` page states that receipt capture, approval thresholds and mobile access are hypotheses, not customer signal, and must not be written into a brief as customer signal. This call changes that for all three, and not uniformly:

- **Approval thresholds — now stated customer signal.** Asked for explicitly by name and number by Their Payroll Administrator ($250 auto-pay), contested on substance by Their HR Manager, and resolved on the call into a specific shape (pays automatically; manager informed via a list, not an email). An existing $5,000 owner tier is in force today.
- **Mobile access — now stated customer signal, and two-sided.** Submission on a phone requested by Their Payroll Administrator; approval on a phone opposed by Their HR Manager with first-hand evidence from their own time-off rollout, and with a *block, not warn* requirement above the threshold. This bears directly on what VP1 shipped mobile-first.
- **Receipt capture — the hypothesis is redirected, not simply confirmed.** No one asked for receipt attachment as such; it is assumed table stakes. What they supplied is the **shape**: receipts here are PDFs and forwarded confirmation emails, with paper photos a small minority of one month's claims. The paper-photography framing carried over from the multi-site design partners does not describe this segment.
- **The A/P-versus-payroll boundary is a segment split, not a fact.** VP2 currently treats "claims run to accounts payable" as the fact to design against. At 75 employees with no A/P function, reimbursement rides the pay run and speed is a non-issue. VP2 has to support both destinations rather than pick one.

**UX Findings**
- **Claim form (Excel on a shared drive):** prior-year copies persist on desktops and arrive with stale mileage rates, forcing the administrator to redo the calculation.<br><br>*"So I get claims on the 2024 form with the old mileage rate on it and I have to redo the math." — Their Payroll Administrator*
- **Submission routing:** no canonical path — some claimants email the administrator directly, some route via their manager first, determined by who trained them.<br><br>*"depending on who taught them, which is not consistent." — Their Payroll Administrator*
- **Approval notification channel:** an FYI email is functionally invisible to a manager at this volume; an in-product monthly list is the requested shape.<br><br>*"a 'for your information' email is a deleted email." — Their HR Manager*
- **Pay statement:** the reimbursement appears as a single unlabelled amount with no claim breakdown, generating repeat desk visits.<br><br>*"It says 'expenses, three hundred and forty dollars.' And then somebody comes to my desk and says, what's the three-forty, and I have to go back to their email from three weeks ago." — Their Payroll Administrator*
- **Self-service discoverability:** a live self-service surface is bypassed in favour of asking a person, four years after being told it exists.<br><br>*"They've been told it's there for four years. At some point it's not a discoverability problem, it's that people would rather ask a person." — Their Payroll Administrator*

**Key Product Gaps**
- **No auto-approval floor:** every claim, down to a $19 parking receipt, waits on a manager click that is not read — described as a *"fake control that costs me three days of waiting."*
- **No manager spend visibility separate from approval:** the only channel that tells a manager what their team spent is the approval email, so removing the approval removes the awareness.
- **Recurring small spend is structurally invisible:** eleven personal-card subscriptions, ~$400/month, approved once 22 months ago and never re-decided. Implied gap — no surface aggregates repeated claims from the same claimant/vendor.
- **No system-owned submission reminder:** the 60-day rule is unenforceable because enforcement is a person, and that person eats the relationship cost.
- **No claim detail on the pay statement:** implied cost of 5–6 desk enquiries per pay across both attendees (she believed it was 2–3 until the HR Manager said *"I just forward them to you and you think it started with you"*).
- **No digital-receipt attach path:** claims arrive as PDFs and forwarded confirmation emails stapled to an Excel file — the capture problem here is attaching and matching digital artifacts, not photographing paper.
- **No employee-facing claim history:** implied gap — every "where is my money / what was that amount" question routes to one of two people.
- **Reimbursement destination is assumed:** the module has to land an approved claim on the pay run for this segment and on accounts payable for larger multi-site clients.

## Insights / Learnings

### How a 75-Employee Back Office Actually Runs Expenses

One person owns the entire claim lifecycle alongside payroll, benefits and year-end. Every requirement below has to survive that: there is nobody to configure a system and nobody to enforce a rule.

| Insight | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Expenses are one job of many, held by one person** | The Payroll Administrator runs payroll, expenses, benefits enrolment, T4s and what little A/P exists. There is no finance function to hand anything to.<br><br>*"Just me. I do payroll, I do expenses, I do the benefits enrolment, I do the T4s." — Their Payroll Administrator* |
| **The cost is time and chasing, not error** | A day and a half a month, half of it chasing people who submit four months in one go — and it is the same three people every time.<br><br>*"It's not that the receipt's hard to get, it's that somebody hasn't submitted anything since May and then in August I get four months in one go." — Their Payroll Administrator* |
| **Volume is low and lumpy** | ~25 claims and ~$6,000 a month from 15–20 of 75 staff; one conference month at ~$14,000, with a single $4,000 trip claim.<br><br>*"That's a quarter of my monthly volume in a single email." — Their Payroll Administrator* |
| **Rules without a system enforcer are decorative** | The 60-day submission rule has never once been enforced, because the only enforcement available is refusing to reimburse a colleague.<br><br>*"So the rule's decorative." — Their Payroll Administrator* |

### The Threshold Disagreement — Approval Versus Visibility

The most valuable exchange on the call. Two roles wanted opposite things, and the resolution reframes what an approval step is *for* at this company size. Michelle called it *"the most useful ten minutes I've had this month"*; the attendees noted they have it roughly weekly.

| Insight | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **The administrator wants a $250 auto-pay floor** | Below the floor the claim pays without approval; above it, a manager sees four claims a month instead of twenty and might actually read them.<br><br>*"Auto-approved. Because right now a manager is clicking yes on a nineteen dollar parking receipt, and they're not reading it. Nobody reads a nineteen dollar parking receipt." — Their Payroll Administrator* |
| **The HR Manager's objection is about awareness, not control** | She concedes managers do not read; she argues they still *see*, and that peripheral signal is what tells her something about a person's workload.<br><br>*"They're not reading, but they are seeing. That's the real reason. The thing the manager gets out of that email isn't a control, it's information." — Their HR Manager* |
| **Tiers cost more than they return at this size** | A threshold is a rule someone has to know, and nobody at 75 people owns knowing rules.<br><br>*"At seventy-five, the manager already knows what their four people are doing. Adding a tier doesn't add oversight, it adds a thing to configure." — Their HR Manager* |
| **They converged on "told, not asked"** | Separating the money decision from the information flow satisfied both roles on the call, with one condition attached.<br><br>*"That works for me too, if it genuinely doesn't hold the money." — Their Payroll Administrator* |
| **Today's real policy is one approval plus a dormant $5,000 tier** | The owner tier has fired exactly twice, ever — the conference and a trade-show booth.<br><br>*"Over five thousand it goes to the owner but that has happened, what, twice? Ever?" — Their HR Manager* |

### Receipts Are Digital, and Invisible Recurring Spend Is the Real Leak

| Insight | Details |

### Mobile — Submit Yes, Approve No, and "Blocked" Rather Than "Discouraged"

The clearest two-sided mobile signal in the corpus, and it lands squarely on what VP1 shipped mobile-first.

## Slack Summary

Great expense discovery call with Acme Corp today — first record we've ever filed for this account, and it is dense. Full recap here: `product/customers/accounts/acme-corp/calls/summaries/2026-08-20.md`

**The threshold disagreement is the headline, and it resolved on the call**
Their Payroll Administrator wants a $250 auto-approval floor: *"under that, it just goes. No approval. Straight to me, straight onto the pay."* Their HR Manager pushed back hard, and with the better argument — the approval email isn't a control, it's awareness: *"They're not reading, but they are seeing… The thing the manager gets out of that email isn't a control, it's information."* Reframing it as approval-versus-visibility landed for both: under $250 it pays, and the manager is **told rather than asked**. Her condition on the telling: not email. *"I'd want it as a list… 'here's what your team spent this month' and I look at it once."* That list also solves their subscription leak — eleven personal-card subscriptions, ~$400/month, approved once 22 months ago and never revisited.

**Reimbursement runs through payroll here, not A/P**
*"On the pay. It's always been on the pay."* No real A/P function; semi-monthly cadence bounds the wait at two weeks, and speed is explicitly a non-issue. That is the opposite of Northwind, and the VP2 page currently names the A/P route as the fact to design against. VP2 needs both destinations.

**Mobile: submit yes, approve no — and blocked, not warned**
Submission on a phone is wanted at the moment of spend. Mobile approval is opposed on their own evidence: *"We put time-off approvals on the phone and the approval rate went to basically a hundred percent overnight, and it wasn't because everybody's requests got better."* She wants large claims hard-blocked on mobile — *"'discouraged' means a grey box that says 'are you sure' and everyone taps yes on those too."* This lands on what VP1 shipped mobile-first, so worth a look.

**Receipts are digital here — that corrects an assumption we were carrying**
*"Hardly ever paper… Everything else was a PDF or somebody forwarding me the confirmation email."* Roughly three paper photos in a month of claims. The photograph-paper-in-a-truck framing is a multi-site pattern, not a universal one.

**Why they didn't buy a standalone tool**
About $9/head, ~$8,000/year for 75 people. *"a day of my time is not eight thousand dollars"* — and the real killer: *"I'd still be exporting it into the payroll to get it onto the pay, so I'd have bought a second system and kept the manual step."* Their HR Manager on the alternative: *"if it's part of what we already pay you for… that's a completely different conversation."* Direct support for the attach-rate motion.

Smaller items raised:
1. System-owned submission reminder at ~day 15 — their 60-day rule has never been enforced, and she doesn't want to be the nag
2. Pay statement should say which claims a reimbursement covers — 5-6 desk enquiries a pay
3. Employee expense history in self-service (asked by their HR Manager, and doubted by their Payroll Administrator on four years of vacation-balance evidence)

Both attendees agreed to a 30-minute rough-prototype review — Dean is booking it for early October. Feature-request records filed for all seven asks.
