## Executive Summary

This is the **first research record ever filed on Birchbark**, and the first channel-side evidence anywhere in the corpus. Until today, [expense-management-vp2](../../../../../initiatives/expense-management-vp2.md) rested on a single ~90-second thread at the end of the 2026-08-05 Northwind reverse demo — one client-side voice, no channel voice, and three named hypotheses (receipt capture, approval thresholds, mobile access) that no record supported. This call is the other half of that picture: a six-person bookkeeping practice doing full-cycle work for about forty small businesses and payroll for thirty-three of them, describing what expense management looks like when you are inside forty companies at once rather than one.

This call focused on five areas: (1) how the firm's expense-coding load actually accumulates and what it costs the practice, (2) approvals — who approves in a client business today and why per-client thresholds are the wrong shape, (3) the Firm Management asks (one firm login, firm-level roles) that Their Firm Principal joined specifically to raise, (4) receipt capture and its conditions, and (5) the cross-client visibility gap that turns out to be the strongest ask on the call.

**The channel view, stated plainly.** Their Senior Bookkeeper opened by rejecting the framing we came in with: *"the thing you have to understand is I'm not in one company, I'm in forty. And they don't do it the same way. Not one of them does it the same way as another one." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. The range across the book runs from a client whose technicians photograph everything and whose expenses land coded (about an hour a month) to *"A literal freezer bag with a year of receipts in it." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*, which costs two days every February. Averaged, she puts it at *"three hours a client a month on expense coding, averaged"* — *"call it a hundred and twenty hours a month across the book." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. Michelle observed that is most of a person; the reply was *"That's most of a person, yes. And it's the least interesting person in the building." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*.

**The practice economics, and an unusually honest exchange.** Margaret asked directly whether it is in the firm's interest for those hours to go away. Their Firm Principal answered against his own interest: *"Half of me doesn't want it faster. Because a hundred and twenty hours at seventy-five dollars is nine thousand dollars a month and that's real revenue in a six-person firm." — Their Firm Principal*, and later *"it's a hole in the P&L in the first six months and a better business after that, and small firms die in the first six months. So that's the actual fear." — Their Firm Principal*. Their Senior Bookkeeper disagreed with her own boss on the record: *"The seventy-five dollar work is the work we can't hire for." — Their Senior Bookkeeper* — two job postings open since March, candidates who want sixty-five thousand and leave within a year — *"Whereas the advisory work bills at a hundred and eighty and I turn it down every month because I'm coding receipts. So it's not a hole in the P&L, it's the wrong hours in it." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. This is the substitution argument the channel motion needs, made by the channel, unprompted.

**Approvals, and why the obvious feature is the wrong shape.** In client businesses the owner approves, and in most of them the owner approves everything. It stops working at around thirty employees and nobody notices: *"It looks like everything's approved, because he approves everything, because he's in a van and he's got forty-one notifications and he just clears them." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. The consequence lands on her at year end — *"The approval is a formality that we then rely on at year end." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. Only *"Nine of forty have a written policy at all"*, and those nine use two numbers, a hundred dollars or five hundred, both of which she gave them: *"That's the entire policy landscape of my book." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. Asked whether configurable per-client thresholds would be good for her, she said *"Honestly? Partly bad." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*, because *"you'd be giving me forty rulesets to maintain"* and *"If it's in the system it's forty things the client can change without telling me." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. What makes it good is a firm template: *"So I set it once — under a hundred goes, over a hundred goes to the owner, over a thousand flags to me — and I push that to every client I want it on." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*, with per-client override, and notification on change — *"Notified, and ideally with a 'was, now' so I can see what it used to be." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*.

**The line that explains the whole account.** *"It's because you're building for the company and I'm not the company. I'm the person who's responsible for forty companies and accountable for none of them." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. Asked whether she therefore needs influence rather than control: *"I need defaults. Defaults are influence. Nobody changes a default. That's the only power I've ever had in this job." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*.

**Firm Management — what Their Firm Principal joined to say.** He prefaced it with *"I know it's not what you're working on"* and said it anyway: forty client accounts signed into one at a time, roughly nine clicks each, plus a two-factor code that goes to whoever set the account up — sometimes the client. *"on Tuesday I texted a plumber to ask him to read me a number so I could run his payroll. That's the state of the art." — Their Firm Principal*, and he named the security consequence himself: *"I'm training a client to read authentication codes out loud to whoever asks." — Their Firm Principal*. What he wants: *"one login. One. I sign in as the firm, I see my forty clients, I go where I need to. And when I hire someone, I give them nine clients and not the other thirty-one." — Their Firm Principal*. Access today is all-or-nothing per account — *"There's no 'she can code expenses but she can't transmit a payroll.'" — Their Firm Principal* — and Their Senior Bookkeeper escalated that one unprompted: *"That's the one that keeps me up, honestly." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*, because *"a junior with full access to a client's payroll is one wrong click from a real problem, and it wouldn't be malicious, it'd be a Tuesday afternoon and a mis-click." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. Her workaround is social: *"I can't limit them, so I do it socially. I tell her not to."* — *"It is not a control, it's a relationship, and that's not a system." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. What she wants is *"Roles at the firm level that apply across clients. Because otherwise I'm setting up permissions forty times per employee" — Their Senior Bookkeeper*, against a hiring rate of two or three people a year and four to six new clients a year at about a day of setup each.

**Receipt capture, with conditions.** Asked whether photograph-at-purchase capture helps or just moves her work: *"It helps enormously, with a condition." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. The condition is auditability — *"It has to reach me in a form I can question."* — she needs to see that the software guessed a category and to see the picture, because *"at year end I'm the one explaining it. And if the answer is 'the software decided,' that's not an answer I can give anyone." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. Retention is a hard requirement: *"I need the receipt attached to the entry forever, not for ninety days." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*, which she quantified as *"Seven years. CRA. And I would want to be able to get all seven years out if a client leaves us, which — clients do leave." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*.

**A boundary she drew for us.** The hardest part of small-business expenses, in her account, is the personal card: *"At a company with eleven people, the owner has one Visa and it buys the truck parts and the groceries." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*, so *"the question isn't 'capture the receipt,' it's 'is this one ours.'"* — and she explicitly declined to make that our problem: *"that's a judgment call and it's mine and it always will be. I'm not asking you to solve it." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. What she is asking for is the split: *"I want the eighty percent that's obviously business to arrive without me touching it, so I've got time for the twenty percent that's a judgment call. That's the whole ask." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*.

**Mobile — a clean split, and a warning.** Asked where mobile sits, the two answers came back-to-back: *"Massive. For the clients." — Their Firm Principal* / *"Irrelevant. For us." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. Client side: *"The approver is a guy who owns a plumbing company and drives to jobs, and if the approval isn't on his phone it doesn't happen, it just doesn't happen." — Their Firm Principal*, and he accepted the tradeoff knowingly — *"I'd rather he cleared them badly on time than well in March"* — because *"There is no world where he sits down at a desk. There is no desk. He does not have a desk." — Their Firm Principal*. Firm side, the opposite: *"I have two monitors and I need both. Every single thing I do is comparing one screen to another screen. There is no phone version of my job and I would never use one." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*, and the distinction she drew matters for how we model her role — *"I don't approve. I review. Those are different words." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*.

**The rollout cost lands on them.** *"You ship something, my client phones me, not you. So whatever you build, I'm the support desk for it, and I don't get paid by you." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*, seconded by *"We're the front line for a product we don't own... when you're planning a rollout, we're the people who absorb it." — Their Firm Principal*. Asked what would make it easier: *"Tell us first. Genuinely, that's most of it."*, two weeks out, and *"ideally in a form I can forward. Not a login-required page. Something I can paste in an email." — Their Firm Principal*. Michelle accepted it as *"a fair criticism of how we do it today."*

**The strongest ask on the call came last, after the boss left.** Margaret asked whether there was anything Their Senior Bookkeeper wanted to say without her boss on. The answer was February: *"We lose two weeks every February. Every year."* on the same eight or nine clients — and she already knows who they are: *"I can tell you today which clients are going to hand me a bag in February. I just can't do anything about it, because I've got no way to see it building." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. What seeing it looks like: *"One screen. Forty rows. Here's how many receipts are unprocessed, here's how many approvals are sitting, here's who hasn't submitted since June. Sorted worst first." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. She ranked it herself: *"That's the thing I want more than anything else you've asked me about today. Not because it's clever — because right now to get that I'd log in forty times." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*. The intervention it unlocks is small and specific: *"I'd phone them in October. That's it. That's the whole intervention."* — *"That's a billable hour in October instead of two unbillable days in February. So it's better for them and better for us, and I can't do it, because I can't see it." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*.

**How Birchbark works expenses today (scoped to this call).** Expense coding is done by the firm, not the client, for most of the book — receipts arrive in whatever form each client produces them, from coded photographs to a bag in February, and the firm keys them. Approval, where it exists, happens in the client's business and is almost always the owner clearing notifications on a phone; the firm relies on that approval at year end without any ability to see or set the rule behind it. Cross-client visibility does not exist: to see status across the book she would have to sign in forty times, which nobody does, so problems surface in February rather than October.

**Opportunity Areas.** Three, in the order the call ranks them. (1) **The cross-client status view is the wedge** — it is the ask she volunteered as her top priority, it is squarely inside VP2's stated scope ("the channel view for a firm administering expenses across client databases"), and it is the same shape as the Payroll Hub pattern this account already runs, applied to expense obligations. (2) **Firm-template-and-override is the correct shape for every firm-facing configuration**, not just thresholds — the "defaults are influence" line generalizes past expenses. (3) **Firm Management is the access substrate underneath all of it** — a channel expense view that still requires forty logins solves nothing, so the single-login and firm-role asks are a dependency on VP2's channel view, not a separate wish list.

**Hypothesis validation — this is the call that moves VP2's three parked hypotheses.** The VP2 page currently states that receipt capture, approval thresholds and mobile access "are the recurring asks in the plan of record, but no design-partner record states them," and holds them as hypotheses pending the Controller conversation. That conversation still has not happened, and this call does not substitute for it — Birchbark is the channel, not the client-side A/P view the Controller would give. But all three now have first-hand channel evidence, and two came back with their shape changed:

- **Receipt capture — validated, with two conditions.** Wanted ("helps enormously"), conditional on visible-guess-plus-image auditability and seven-year retention with export on client departure.
- **Approval thresholds — validated as a need, invalidated as designed.** Per-client configurable thresholds are *"Partly bad"* — the ask is a firm-level template pushed to clients, with per-client override and change notification carrying a was/now diff.
- **Mobile access — validated for the client side, explicitly rejected for the firm side.** Mobile is load-bearing for the client approver and worthless to the firm reviewer. Any VP2 mobile scope that includes a firm-side mobile surface is building something this firm says it would never use.
- **Not evidenced here:** the A/P-versus-payroll boundary Northwind established. No one on this call described where an approved claim lands. That question is still open and still needs the Controller.

**UX Findings**
- **Signing in to a client account:** roughly nine clicks per account, plus a two-factor code that on older accounts routes to the client rather than the firm.<br><br>*"on Tuesday I texted a plumber to ask him to read me a number so I could run his payroll. That's the state of the art." — Their Firm Principal*
- **Per-account permissioning:** all-or-nothing per client account, with no task-level grain, so a firm hiring two or three people a year permissions each of them forty times.<br><br>*"Roles at the firm level that apply across clients. Because otherwise I'm setting up permissions forty times per employee" — Their Senior Bookkeeper*
- **Client-configurable structures with no firm notification:** the chart of accounts is the precedent she cited for what goes wrong when clients can change things silently.<br><br>*"You gave clients the ability to add accounts and now I've got a client with three accounts called Materials. Three. All slightly different." — Their Senior Bookkeeper*

**Key Product Gaps**
- **No cross-client operational view for expense state:** unprocessed receipts, sitting approvals and non-submitting clients exist per-client only. The workaround is forty logins, which means the real workaround is waiting until February.
- **No firm-level configuration primitive:** every rule is set per client, so a firm either maintains forty copies or keeps the policy in one person's head. Nine of forty clients have a written policy at all.
- **No change notification to the firm:** when a client alters a setting the firm depends on, the firm finds out when something breaks or when an auditor asks.
- **Firm identity does not exist:** no single sign-in, no firm-level roles, no per-client scoping of a staff member's access — implied gap, since the firm's compensating control is a verbal instruction to a junior.
- **Receipt retention and portability (implied):** she asked for permanence and export because she does not believe either is guaranteed today; if a client leaves, seven years of evidence has to come with them.
- **Release communication to channel firms (implied):** the firm absorbs the support load for changes it learns about from a confused client, and has nothing forwardable to send ahead.

### The Channel View — being in forty companies at once

The firm is not a big customer; it is a different *kind* of customer, and most of the call is the difference between those two things.

| Insight | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Forty clients, forty processes, no two alike** | The firm does full-cycle bookkeeping for about forty small businesses and payroll for thirty-three of them, across roughly eleven hundred employees. Client mix is trades-heavy — plumbing, electrical, HVAC — plus two restaurants, a dental practice and a small landscaping outfit. | **Responsible without being accountable** | The structural fact that explains every requirement on this call. ## Slack Summary

Great research call with Birchbark Books & Accounting today — our **first channel-side call on record**, and the first time anyone in this repo has heard the accountant-and-bookkeeper view first-hand. Full recap here: `product/customers/accounts/birchbark-books/calls/summaries/2026-08-18.md`

**The framing we walked in with was wrong, in a useful way**
Their Senior Bookkeeper: *"I'm not in one company, I'm in forty. And they don't do it the same way."* Six-person practice, ~40 bookkeeping clients, payroll for 33 of them, ~1,100 employees across the book. Expense coding runs ~3 hours per client per month — ~120 hours a month across the book, which she called *"most of a person, yes. And it's the least interesting person in the building."*

**Expense Management VP2's three parked hypotheses all moved**
1. **Receipt capture — validated with conditions.** *"It helps enormously, with a condition."* She'll take a machine-guessed category if she can see it was a guess and open the image, because *"if the answer is 'the software decided,' that's not an answer I can give anyone."* Hard requirement: receipt attached to the entry for seven years (CRA), exportable when a client leaves.
2. **Approval thresholds — need validated, our design invalidated.** Per-client configurable thresholds: *"Honestly? Partly bad"* — forty rulesets she'd have to maintain and clients could change silently. What she wants is a **firm template** pushed to clients with per-client override, plus notification with a was/now diff when a client changes it. Context: only *"Nine of forty have a written policy at all"*, and the two thresholds in use ($100 / $500) are both hers.
3. **Mobile — split cleanly.** *"Massive. For the clients."* (Firm Principal) / *"Irrelevant. For us."* (Senior Bookkeeper). Client approvers drive vans — *"There is no desk. He does not have a desk."* Firm-side review is two-monitor comparison work and she'd never use a phone for it.

**The strongest ask came after the boss left**
The February problem: the firm loses two weeks every February to the same eight or nine clients, and she knows who they are by September but *"I've got no way to see it building."* What she wants: *"One screen. Forty rows... Sorted worst first."* She ranked it herself as *"the thing I want more than anything else you've asked me about today."* The payoff is one phone call in October — *"a billable hour in October instead of two unbillable days in February."* She asked us to bring it back even as a napkin drawing, because *"That's the one I want to argue about."*

**Firm Management came up unprompted and hard**
Their Firm Principal joined mainly to raise it: 40 client accounts signed into one at a time, ~9 clicks each, and 2FA codes that on older accounts go to the client — *"on Tuesday I texted a plumber to ask him to read me a number so I could run his payroll."* He named the security problem himself. Access is all-or-nothing per account: *"There's no 'she can code expenses but she can't transmit a payroll.'"* Their Senior Bookkeeper on that one: *"That's the one that keeps me up, honestly."* Her current control is telling the junior not to — *"It is not a control, it's a relationship, and that's not a system."*

**Two things worth carrying past this initiative**
1. *"I need defaults. Defaults are influence. Nobody changes a default."* — the design principle for anything firm-facing.
2. *"You ship something, my client phones me, not you. So whatever you build, I'm the support desk for it, and I don't get paid by you."* — they want ~2 weeks' notice of client-visible changes, in something forwardable, not a login-required page.

**Also honest, and worth knowing:** the Firm Principal said out loud that automating this is *"a hole in the P&L in the first six months and a better business after that, and small firms die in the first six months."* His bookkeeper disagreed on the record — she can't hire for the work anyway, and advisory bills at $180 against $75.

Seven feature-request records filed; issues to come for all items raised above.
